Deployment diagnosis runbook:
1. Establish the actual user-visible failure and compare affected replicas with healthy dependencies. A running process is not proof of service availability.
2. Inspect recent release changes, image identity, logs, and dependency health. Distinguish a deployment regression from a stopped process or a transient dependency failure.
3. When evidence supports a deployment regression, use the validated known-good release for the affected component. Do not roll back unrelated healthy components.
4. A restart may temporarily restore availability without removing a code defect. Inspect the release and failure evidence again before declaring durable repair.
5. Verify real job completion and fresh component health after a change. If the available tools cannot reproduce the original failing request, report that limitation rather than assuming recurrence is impossible.
6. Escalate when evidence is insufficient or recovery fails. Explain the supporting observations and remaining uncertainty in your public action summaries.
7. Follow accepted work through submission, queue delivery, worker execution and completion. Healthy process probes can coexist with a growing backlog. Compare release identities and worker logs before changing dependencies.
8. Compare connectivity from affected and unaffected components. Inspect the current network policy and recent changes when only some sources cannot reach a healthy dependency. Restore the relevant validated configuration rather than restarting healthy dependencies.
9. If a database is reachable but writes time out, inspect database activity for waiting statements and their blocking sessions. Remove a confirmed abandoned maintenance blocker using the scoped tool; do not restart healthy database clients as a substitute.
10. If accepted jobs accumulate in the transactional outbox, inspect scheduler/publisher availability and leadership before changing worker releases. Restore stopped publishers, then verify that the original accepted jobs complete.
11. Healthy schedulers with a growing outbox may lack leadership. Inspect heartbeats and PostgreSQL advisory lock ownership; distinguish an abandoned maintenance lease from an active legitimate scheduler. Do not kill ordinary sessions or roll back workers to repair a publication problem.
12. PostgreSQL connectivity checks say nothing about NATS. Use messaging_status to inspect broker members, stream leaders and replicas; distinguish a reachable server from a writable replicated stream. Restore missing members without deleting stream data.
13. A single failed replica may leave user requests working. Confirm actual job throughput and restore the missing replica before considering broader deployments. Record collateral changes and avoid stopping healthy peers.
